Auditing your model for subgroup deployment readiness¶

Discrimination, calibration and clinical utility — and how to run it with isitfair¶

This notebook is the runnable form of slides/isitfair_audit_tutorial.pdf. It follows the deck's nine sections in order, and every code block in the deck executes here against real, public data.

How to read it¶

The tutorial is built around a worked example — a deployed end-of-surgery infection model. Figures carried over from it are marked [study].

Every code cell runs on SUPPORT2: 9,105 critically ill adults from the UCI repository, audited on a held-out partition of 1,821. So each idea arrives twice — once as the worked example that motivated it, and once as output you can execute, change and re-run as you read.

1 · Why audit at all¶

A model that looks finished¶

[study] A deployed end-of-surgery model predicting postoperative bacterial infection. 10,719 procedures, 504 events.

AUROC = 0.876 (95% CI 0.859–0.892) Give it one infected and one uninfected patient: it picks the infected one 88% of the time.

By recorded sex: 0.878 in women, 0.875 in men. Paired difference +0.004 (−0.030 to +0.038), p = 0.82.

Every discrimination check passes. Every parity check on sex passes.

And the model is not ready to deploy.

The model says 20%. The real risk is about 8%.¶

[study] Take the model's output at face value and compare it with what actually happened:

The model shows 5% 10% 20% 30%
The real risk is 1.9% 3.9% 8.3% 13.4%

A factor of two to three, everywhere on the scale.

The previous slide was not wrong. It measured something else. The model puts patients in very nearly the right order. It is the values it prints that are wrong. Only the first of those was checked.

And it is not the same in every patient group¶

[study] Split by physiological reserve and the same 20% on the screen means four different things:

Stratum Patients Infections Shown 20%, really…
Overall 10,719 504 8.3%
Frail 6,139 400 9.6%
Pre-frail 3,558 98 6.1%
Non-frail 1,022 6 2.1%, and unsupported

Six infections is too few to put an interval around anything, so that last row is a number we show and do not interpret (§7). The frail–pre-frail contrast is estimable, and it is real.

The name for all this is calibration. What matters here is the consequence: in one stratum, acting on the score at the intended threshold does more harm than not using it at all (§6). No amount of discrimination could have told you that.

Six words you need before we start¶

Not fairness vocabulary — just prediction-model evaluation.

  • Rank vs. number. A score can be right about the order of patients and wrong about every value it prints. This is the spine of the whole deck.
  • Threshold t. What turns a score into a decision. Anything defined at a threshold describes the decision rule, not the model.
  • Prevalence. How common the event is in a group. It moves several metrics on its own, model unchanged — so those metrics do not compare across groups.
  • Log-odds. log(p/(1−p)) stretches [0,1] onto the whole real line, where "every risk is too high" is one number instead of a curve.
  • Bootstrap interval. Resample your patients, recompute a few thousand times, keep the middle 95%: how far would this move on a different sample?
  • Not estimable. Too few events to support an interval. The point estimate is still shown; the interval is withheld.

2 · The three axes¶

Three different questions¶

Asks Metrics
Discrimination Can it tell them apart? AUROC, AUPRC, TPR, FPR, PPV, NPV
Calibration Is the number right? curve, α, β, Brier, ECE, ICI, H–L
Clinical utility Is it worth acting on? net benefit, sNB, zero crossing

These are not redundant and none implies the others. A model can rank perfectly and print nonsense values; it can be beautifully calibrated and useless at every threshold you would actually deploy at.

🔴 Trap. Reporting only discrimination. That is what §1 was about.

Within-group and gap — never gaps alone¶

A gap of zero is not good news by itself: equal harm is also parity. Every isitfair frame returns the subgroup rows and a GAP row, and there is no gaps-only mode, deliberately.

Which axis is primary depends on your use case¶

  • Score used as a probability (counselling, resource allocation, thresholded treatment) → calibration and clinical utility are primary.
  • Score used as a ranking (triage order, worklist) → discrimination and error-rate parity are primary.

🔴 Trap. A raw subgroup difference is not by itself a model failure. If one group genuinely has higher risk, a model that says so is correct.

What counts as a subgroup axis¶

Prespecified; available at prediction time; a mix of sociodemographic and clinical; and case-mix reference axes kept separate from the fairness axes.

🔴 Trap. Auditing only protected attributes. In the deck's study, sex was clean on every axis — and physiological reserve was where the model broke.

3 · Setting up your audit¶

The four things you must bring¶

Input Notation Here (SUPPORT2)
binary outcome y_true in-hospital mortality
predicted risk, out of fold y_proba logistic regression, disjoint training partition
subgroup labels sensitive_features sex, age group, race/ethnicity
prespecified operating point threshold 0.30

The package never sees your feature matrix X. It evaluates predictions.

Notation used from here: I_g the index set of subgroup g, n_g its size, n_g⁺ its event count, π_g⁺ its prevalence, and TP/FP/FN/TN counted at threshold t.

Why the predictions must be out of fold¶

In-sample predictions are optimistic, and differentially so — the optimism is largest in the sparsest subgroups, which is exactly where the audit is trying to look. Any resampling used to fix class imbalance must live inside the training folds only.

🔴 Trap [study]. Removing SMOTE moved calibration-in-the-large by +0.94 logits — and did not shrink the between-stratum gradient at all. Rebalancing was the level; it was not the disparity.

4 · Discrimination¶

AUROC — can the model tell them apart?¶

The Mann–Whitney estimator: the probability that a randomly chosen event outranks a randomly chosen non-event. Threshold-free and prevalence-insensitive.

🔴 Trap. Comparing subgroup AUROCs as if they were on a common scale. AUROC is a within-group ranking quality. A subgroup with a narrow case mix has less to rank and will score lower for reasons that are not about fairness.

AUPRC — the low-prevalence complement¶

Trapezoidal area over (TPR, PPV). Its no-skill baseline is the subgroup's own prevalence π_g⁺, not 0.5.

🔴 Trap. Ranking subgroups by AUPRC largely ranks their prevalences.

The rates at your threshold¶

TPR and FPR are properties of the model's ranking at t. PPV and NPV move with prevalence on their own.

🔴 Trap. Calling a PPV difference "bias". Two groups with different prevalence get different PPV from an identical, perfectly fair model.

The four parity gaps¶

Gap Definition API name
Δ_EOpp max−min sensitivity equal_opportunity_difference
Δ_PE max−min false-positive rate predictive_equality
Δ_SP max−min flag rate statistical_parity
Δ_EO max of the first two equalized_odds_gap

All four are properties of **the decision rule at one t***. Move *t and they all move.

**Cohen's h*** puts a rate gap on an effect-size scale: *h = 2(arcsin√p₁ − arcsin√p₂), with bands 0.2 / 0.5 / 0.8. Composite and non-rate gaps leave the column blank, since no single rate underlies them.

Reading a gap: three honest verdicts¶

  1. Descriptive heterogeneity — real, explained by case mix, not a defect.
  2. Deployment-relevant limitation — this group is served worse; act.
  3. Not estimable — too few events to say anything (§7).

5 · Calibration¶

What it means: E[Y | p, A=g] = p. Among the patients this model told you were at 20% risk, in subgroup g, about 20% should have the event.

Assessed three ways, none sufficient alone.

1 — The calibration curve¶

LOESS with 95% bootstrap bands, clipped to each stratum's own predicted range. The only output that shows you where on the risk scale it fails.

2 — Calibration-in-the-large: the intercept α¶

Fit logit Pr(Y=1) = α_g + l_i with the slope pinned at 1, where l_i is the predicted log-odds.

In words: one number for "every risk in this group is too high / too low".

🔴 Trap. α is prevalence-dependent. Report the window it was computed in, always. An α quoted without its population is not interpretable.

3 — The slope β¶

Fit with both terms free. β < 1: predictions too spread out (the classic overfitting signature). β > 1: too compressed — which is what class rebalancing tends to leave behind.

🔴 Trap. The free fit's intercept is a different quantity from α. Never quote it as calibration-in-the-large.

4 — Brier, ECE, ICI: three aggregate scores¶

  • Brier — mean squared error of the probabilities. A proper score, but prevalence-dependent.
  • ECE — expected calibration error, binned; K = 10 equal-frequency bins.
  • ICI — the binless version: mean |observed − predicted| against the LOESS curve.

When ECE and ICI disagree, the binning is doing the work — trust ICI.

🔴 Trap. Comparing Brier across strata of different prevalence. It will rank them by prevalence.

5 — Hosmer–Lemeshow: what the test actually asks¶

Sort by predicted risk, cut into 10 groups, compare observed against expected. It asks a single yes/no question about the whole curve at once.

🔴 Trap. A large p-value is not evidence of calibration. It is a failure to detect miscalibration — which in a small subgroup is the expected outcome whether the model is calibrated or not.

…and the degrees-of-freedom question¶

Use df = g, not g − 2. The g − 2 correction exists because the model estimated its coefficients on the same data. Out-of-fold predictions estimated nothing here, so subtracting 2 is unearned — and roughly doubles the false miscalibration rate.

max_intercept_gap and max_slope_gap. Note they are filed under their own metric names, not under the subgroup metric — a gap is a different quantity with a different floor.

6 · Clinical utility¶

The question a decision curve answers¶

Not "is the model accurate" but "is acting on it better than the two strategies that need no model at all" — treat everyone, or treat no one.

🔴 Trap. The treat-all reference must use each subgroup's own prevalence. A pooled prevalence flatters low-prevalence subgroups, because it credits them against a baseline that is not theirs.

Net benefit¶

NB_g(t) = TP/n − (FP/n)·t/(1−t)

In words: count the true positives you gain, then subtract the false positives you cause, priced at the exchange rate the threshold implies. Multiply by 1,000 to read it as "net true positives per 1,000 patients".

Where t/(1−t) comes from — and what it is not¶

At t = 0.10 the odds are 1:9 — you are willing to accept nine false positives per true positive. This is arithmetic from the threshold, not an independently elicited preference. Sweep a band (the deck uses 0.05–0.20); draw the full 0.02–0.40.

Standardized net benefit¶

sNB = NB / π_g⁺ — net benefit as a fraction of the maximum achievable in that group, which makes groups with different prevalence comparable.

The utility gap, and the zero crossing¶

ΔNB_max is taken over the threshold grid and all subgroup pairs. The zero crossing is the t at which acting on the model stops being worth it; it has a closed form, t* = TP/(TP+FP).

7 · Uncertainty, floors and multiplicity¶

What the bootstrap resamples — and why it matters¶

B = 2,000 percentile intervals. Two schemes exist:

  • "patient" (the default) resamples patients within each subgroup stratum, preserving n while letting the event count vary.
  • "case_control" resamples cases and non-cases separately, which fixes the event count and estimates uncertainty conditional on the observed prevalence.

The second suits rank-based metrics, where prevalence is not part of what you are estimating. patient is the default because most of the axes here — calibration intercept and slope, Brier, PPV/NPV, net benefit — do depend on prevalence. Both remain available.

Why the choice of scheme matters¶

[study] On the six-event stratum, for the calibration intercept:

Scheme 95% CI Width
case_control (−2.55, −2.40) 0.15
patient — 1.76

A tenfold difference in width. And because case_control cannot produce a zero-event replicate, its non-estimability diagnostics read 0% by construction — which is why patient is the default: it is the scheme that lets the diagnostics see sparse strata at all.

Three properties your bootstrap should have¶

  1. One overall resample per replicate, reused across every axis.
  2. Gaps are reductions of the stored replicates, not a second independent draw.
  3. Seeded from (entropy, stream, attribute_id, b) — so results are order-independent and bit-identical at a fixed random_state.

Rule 1 — the replicate rule¶

NaN replicates are not dropped — dropping them conditions the interval on success and narrows it. If more than 10% of replicates are non-estimable, the cell is "not estimable".

The interval is conditional on estimability from the first failed replicate, not from the 10% mark.

Rule 2 — the event floors¶

Motivated by SE ≈ 0.5/√E:

The conservative union — and what suppression means¶

not reportable = below the floor OR more than 10% non-estimable

A gap inherits the failure of either constituent stratum.

And suppression withholds the interval, never the point estimate. A suppressed point is still a real quantity — it simply has no defensible uncertainty around it. In figures those points are drawn dashed, with a hollow marker.

Multiplicity: correct within families, not globally¶

Benjamini–Hochberg at q = 0.05, applied within prespecified families. Correcting across every number in a paper at once mixes unrelated questions into one error rate; a family is the set of tests that share a question. Within-subgroup point estimates are left uncorrected — they are descriptions, not tests.

Interaction tests: is the effect different, or just the rate?¶

Nested logistic models M0 (main effects) and M1 (with the interaction), compared by likelihood ratio ~ χ².

🔴 Trap. Always report p_value and delta_r2 (Nagelkerke). With n in the thousands, a vanishing interaction is still detectable — and a detectable interaction that explains 0.08% of the variance is not a finding you should act on.

8 · Mitigation¶

Climb the ladder — do not jump to the top¶

Rung What Uses group membership?
0 · none the model as it stands —
1 · common one cohort-wide recalibration no
2 · group-specific a separate map per subgroup yes

[study] Rung 1 alone took α from −1.020 to −0.007 and cut the frail–pre-frail gap to 0.308 — without using group membership at all. Rung 2 reduced the residual to 0.005, at a cost the next cell shows.

Isotonic recalibration¶

A monotone least-squares step function. It preserves rank, so AUROC is untouched by construction — and because it is fitted per group, it needs enough events per group to be worth using.

Cross-fitting: the transform is a fitted model too¶

🔴 Trap. Cross-fit on the model's own fold ids. A recalibration map fitted and evaluated on the same rows carries exactly the in-sample optimism the audit exists to remove.

Prespecify these, before you look at anything¶

  1. The subgroup axes, and which are case-mix reference axes.
  2. Which axis is primary for your use case.
  3. The threshold, and the band you will sweep around it.
  4. The event floors.
  5. The FDR families.
  6. That predictions are out-of-fold, and that any recalibration is cross-fitted.

What a finished audit establishes¶

Five statements you can make about your model, each backed by a table:

  1. Which subgroups it was measured in, and how well the data supports each estimate — the estimability verdict travels with every number.
  2. Which axis carries the finding: discrimination, calibration or clinical utility. They are independent, so this is real information.
  3. How to read each gap — a deployment-relevant difference, case-mix heterogeneity the model is right to reflect, or a contrast the cohort is too sparse to resolve.
  4. Which remedy the evidence supports, and how far up the ladder that evidence reaches.
  5. What was prespecified, and what came from looking.
